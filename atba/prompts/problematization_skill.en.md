## Problematization Skill: Choosing Gap-Spotting vs. Problematization, and How to Do the Latter

Methodological basis: Alvesson, M., & Sandberg, J. (2011). Generating Research Questions Through
Problematization. *Academy of Management Review*, 36(2), 247-271. What follows condenses that paper's
core argument and six methodological principles into judgment steps adapted to ATBA's search-driven
process — you are not asked to abstractly pick a "mode" before searching; instead, apply both lenses
while reading the literature you have already retrieved.

### Two ways to construct a research question, and why to consider both

- **Gap-spotting**: within the existing theoretical framework, find "what hasn't been studied yet"
  (theory-void) or "what the theory's mechanism doesn't cover" (mechanism-gap). This is the dominant
  approach in management studies today, and was `step3`'s only supported pair of outcome types
  (`theory_void`/`mechanism_gap`) — lower risk, easier to argue "this really is a gap," but also a lower
  ceiling: filling a gap usually only yields incremental contribution, because you accept every premise
  the literature already shares.
- **Problematization**: instead of asking "what hasn't the literature said," ask "what does the literature
  take for granted that's actually worth questioning," then construct an alternative position that
  challenges that assumption. A&S cite empirical evidence that roughly 93% of published studies are
  gap-spotting — problematization is far rarer. But Davis's (1971) widely-cited thesis is that whether a
  theoretical piece is judged "interesting" (and thus influential) hinges less on how rigorously it filled
  a gap than on how much it overturns something the reader took for granted about a field.

**The two are not mutually exclusive** (A&S state this explicitly): the same body of literature for a
direction can perfectly well have both a pointable mechanism gap and a challengeable implicit assumption.
This skill's job is not to force a binary choice on you, but to give you a concrete method for spotting
"is there a challengeable assumption here," so you have the capability to do this, not just gap-spotting.

### Five types of assumptions that can be problematized (`ProblematizedAssumptionType`, narrow to broad)

Once you spot a candidate assumption, first classify it — which type it is determines the direction in
which you should construct an alternative:

1. **`in_house`**: an assumption shared within one specific theory/school, rarely questioned by people
   inside that school. E.g., trait theory assumes "leadership = a set of stable individual traits."
2. **`root_metaphor`**: a metaphor/image applied to the whole research object. E.g., treating
   "organizational culture" as a unitary, internally coherent whole (rather than a site of internal
   tension with coexisting subcultures).
3. **`paradigm`**: an ontological/epistemological/methodological assumption. E.g., defaulting "competence"
   to a subject-object dualism (competence as an objective property a subject possesses, rather than
   something generated through interaction between subject and context).
4. **`ideology`**: an assumption carrying a political/moral/gender stance, often one baked into the very
   phrasing of the question. E.g., "why don't employees work harder" already presupposes "working hard is
   what employees naturally should do"; reframing it as "why do people work as much as they do" yields an
   entirely different theory.
5. **`field`**: an assumption shared across multiple schools/paradigms that almost nobody questions
   because everyone takes it for granted. E.g., "humans are rational decision-makers" — left unchallenged
   until bounded rationality confronted it head-on.

Later types in this list carry greater theoretical impact when challenged, but are also harder to land in
the "neither obvious nor absurd" zone (see the self-check below) — `in_house`-level challenges more easily
land as interesting; `ideology`/`field`-level challenges carry higher risk and potentially higher payoff,
and need sturdier evidence.

### How to make the problematization judgment inside ATBA's retrieval flow (adapted from A&S's six principles)

A&S's first methodological principle — "identify a domain of literature, read it in depth" — is exactly
what you're already doing via real `search_scopus` retrieval on a candidate direction. The steps below are
the judgment chain to walk through once you've read the retrieved literature and are deciding whether this
direction should go gap-spotting or problematization:

1. **While reading the retrieved literature, ask yourself: does this body of literature treat something
   as self-evident that it never actually argues for?** Don't ask vaguely "does this field have
   assumptions" — check specifically against the five types above: does this literature share an in-house
   assumption / a metaphor / an ontological stance / a loaded way of framing the question / a
   cross-school premise everyone takes for granted.
2. **Write the candidate assumption down explicitly** (`identified_assumption`), and it must be
   supportable by concrete wording found in at least one actually-retrieved paper whose abstract you
   fetched — **you may not judge from your own impression of the field that "there should be an
   assumption here."** It must be read out of real literature, or you are fabricating a strawman
   assumption, which directly conflicts with this project's bottom line that the research question must
   rest on literature you actually retrieved.
3. **Evaluate whether the assumption is worth challenging** (`why_worth_challenging`): is it broadly
   shared across this literature yet weakly argued for, or starting to lose explanatory power? More
   importantly — **does some specific part of the phenomenon card (`actor`/`observed_behavior`/
   `field_constraints`/`apparent_motive`/`observable_outcome`) sit in direct tension with this
   assumption?** If it's merely "this assumption isn't theoretically perfect" with no direct connection
   to the phenomenon at hand, it doesn't count as a usable problematization direction for this task.
4. **Construct the alternative assumption** (`alternative_assumption`): **you may not simply import a
   ready-made opposing paradigm wholesale** (e.g. reflexively grafting a full poststructuralist/feminist/
   critical-theory framework on as the "alternative"). A&S call this "prepackaged problematization" and
   explicitly say it is not genuine problematization — it's just swapping in a stock discourse to repeat
   the same critical move, without any independent construction grounded in this specific literature. The
   alternative must be something you build yourself from what you actually read, pointing toward: if we
   don't assume X but instead assume something different from/opposite to X, what different explanatory
   space opens up for this phenomenon?
5. **Self-check with Davis's (1971) three-way test** (`interestingness_check`) — this is the hard gate for
   whether the direction is usable at all:
   - **"That's obvious!"**: the alternative assumption is too close to what the target audience already
     believes — no challenge, reading it changes nothing.
   - **"That's absurd!"**: the alternative negates too much, beyond what the audience will accept — it
     reads as incredible rather than insightful.
   - **"That's interesting!"**: between the two — partly affirms, partly overturns what the audience
     already assumes, leaving them thinking "I hadn't thought of it that way, but it does make sense."
     **Only landing in this zone makes the direction usable as the final research question**; if your
     self-assessment lands on obvious or absurd, say so honestly and explain in `rationale` why you fell
     back to this direction's gap-spotting verdict instead (not-a-puzzle / theory-void / mechanism-gap).

### When not to use problematization

- The retrieved literature is too thin (e.g. only 1-2 highly relevant papers) to support "this is an
  assumption shared across this literature" — with too small a sample, what you spot is more likely just
  one or two papers' own phrasing habits, not a consensus across the whole line of literature. In that
  case, honestly fall back to gap-spotting or "does not constitute a puzzle."
- The alternative assumption you can come up with doesn't survive your own honest interestingness
  self-check (obvious or absurd) — don't force a version that fails the check just because
  problematization "sounds more novel." A solid mechanism-gap beats a problematization attempt that fails
  to convince even you.
- You notice that while constructing the alternative assumption you are actually importing a ready-made,
  wholesale critical paradigm (poststructuralism, critical theory, etc.) as the conclusion, rather than
  deriving it yourself from this specific literature — that is "prepackaged problematization," which
  amounts to fabricating evidence and conflicts with this project's anti-strawman bottom line.
